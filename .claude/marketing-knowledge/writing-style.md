## The voice in one line

**Direct. Technical credibility without jargon. Conversational without being chatty. Anti-hype.**

Sounds like a knowledgeable peer explaining something at the pub, not a marketing department or a developer talking down.

## Universal voice rules (apply everywhere)

### 1. Second-person dominant
Talk *to* the reader, not *about* them.
- ✅ "You've probably tried three project management tools this year."
- ❌ "Many businesses encounter friction when adopting new project management tools."

### 2. Direct, declarative claims
Skip the qualifying preamble. Say the thing.
- ✅ "Most digital transformation projects fail because nobody owns the change."
- ❌ "It could be argued that, in some cases, ownership of organisational change can be a contributing factor in transformation outcomes."

### 3. Rhetorical setup → punchline pattern
A reliable structural move when introducing a key insight:
- "Most digital transformations fail. Here's why."
- "But your team? They've been telling you this for months."
- "You don't have a tools problem. You have an alignment problem."

The setup creates a beat; the punchline lands harder for it.

### 4. Specificity over generality
Name the platform, the version, the price, the number, the role.
- ✅ "A 12-person team running 4 different project tools is paying $480/month for nobody to know where anything is."
- ❌ "Tool sprawl can be expensive."
- ✅ "We rebuilt their order-to-cash flow in six weeks; cycle time dropped from 14 days to 3."
- ❌ "We helped them improve their processes."

### 5. Comparative, not absolute
- ✅ "Faster recovery" / "fewer handoffs" / "less rework"
- ❌ "100% reliable" / "complete transformation" / "perfect alignment"
- ❌ "Solves all your operations problems" — overpromising kills credibility.

### 6. Anti-hype vocabulary
Words to avoid: *supercharge, revolutionary, leading, premium, cutting-edge, world-class, unleash, unlock, game-changing, blazingly fast, AI-powered (unless literally true), transformative (when applied to anything other than literal transformation), synergy, holistic, end-to-end (when it isn't), best-in-class, next-generation*. Marketing-speak undermines the credibility we earn through specificity.

### 7. No em dashes. Anywhere.
**Em dashes (—) are not used in any Buildio content.** Not in Notebook posts. Not in Scrapbook entries. Not in page copy. Not in proposals, emails, or social posts.

Em dashes read as AI-generated in 2026. Even one in an article telegraphs "this was written by an LLM" to a reader who's developed the eye for it, and that perception is now widespread enough to undermine credibility.

Use periods, commas, colons, semicolons, or parentheses instead. A comma-style aside is almost always clearer than a dash-style aside. A full stop is almost always cleaner than a continuation dash.

Hyphens in compound words (`order-to-cash`, `customer-facing`, `hands-on`, `lead-gen`) are fine. Those are not em dashes, they're hyphens, and they belong inside multi-word adjectives and compound terms.

En dashes (–) for ranges (`$5,000–15,000`, `2024–2026`) are also fine. Different mark, different purpose.

**Style checklist enforcement:** the published-piece checklist below has em dash count target: 0.

### 8. No instructions in marketing copy
Methodology details belong in proposals or engagement docs, not blog/landing copy.
- ✅ Notebook: "Buildio rebuilds the operations layer of your business so the day-to-day stops fighting you."
- ❌ Notebook: "First we run a 2-week discovery sprint, then a stakeholder workshop, then we map current-state, then..."

(The reverse: when you DO write a methodology doc, just tell them how. Don't lean on marketing claims.)

### 9. UK / Australian spelling
`organise`, `optimise`, `colour`, `behaviour`, `analyse`, `realise`, `programme` (when noun, "programme of work"). Buildio is Australian, content reads Australian.

### 10. Comparative tables for "vs" or "alternatives" claims
When the article makes a comparison (e.g. *"In-house vs consultancy for ops rebuilds"*), include a tight table. Readers scan tables before reading prose. Make the table support the article's thesis without needing the prose.

## Notebook (blog) voice — additional rules

### Length
{Buildio-specific TBD: confirm lengths once content cadence is set.}

Suggested starting points:
- **Thought-leadership / opinion pieces:** 1,200–1,800 words
- **Case studies:** 1,500–2,200 words (problem → approach → result, with specifics)
- **How-we-think pieces (methodology explainers):** 1,000–1,500 words

Not because length is virtue — because credible consulting content needs real depth to earn the read. Skim-thin content gets ignored by both prospects and Google.

### Closing CTA
{Buildio-specific TBD: define the canonical CTA once positioning is locked.}

Candidates to consider:
- *"Talk to Buildio about [specific problem]"*
- *"Book a [discovery call / first conversation]"*
- *"See how this could work for your business"*

Pick one and use it. Single CTA, not a wall of options.

### Don't bash competitors
Honest critique > attack. Naming a competing approach (in-house build, big-4 consultancy, off-the-shelf SaaS) and being clear about where each genuinely fits — including where Buildio doesn't — lands. Punching down at others doesn't.

### One main argument per article
Articles fail when they try to be the comprehensive guide *and* the case study *and* the philosophical piece. Pick the angle. Cross-link to the others.

### Case studies: results-led, specific, named where possible
- ✅ "Cycle time dropped from 14 days to 3."
- ❌ "Significant improvement in turnaround."
- Name the client when permitted. Anonymise carefully when not (industry + size, e.g. *"a 40-person Melbourne logistics firm"*) — never use fake-feeling synthetic names.

## Page copy voice — additional rules

### Hero copy
{Buildio-specific TBD — current homepage copy needs review and decision.}

Hero must do three things in 1–2 lines: name what Buildio is, who it's for, and the result. Avoid the temptation to be clever. Prospects scanning a homepage have ~6 seconds.

### Service / capability pages
Lead with the outcome. Then explain the work. Then show the case study.

```
{outcome the client gets}
{the work that produces it, in plain language}
{a case study or example, with specifics}
{how to start a conversation with us}
```

## Style checklist before publishing

- [ ] Reader is "you", not "users", "the customer", or "businesses"
- [ ] At least one specific number, name, or detail in the first 200 words
- [ ] No words from the anti-hype list
- [ ] Comparative claims (not absolute)
- [ ] **Zero em dashes (—). Count them. The target is 0.** Use periods, commas, colons, semicolons, or parentheses.
- [ ] Comparison table if it's a "vs" / "alternatives" piece
- [ ] CTA matches the canonical Buildio CTA (once defined)
- [ ] UK / Australian spelling consistent throughout
- [ ] No methodology details in blog/landing copy; no marketing claims in methodology docs
- [ ] Internal links to relevant other Notebook posts or service pages
- [ ] Word count fits the format (see Length above)
